# CV Lab

Interactive computer-vision demonstrations that run entirely in the browser. The site has no backend, build step, or external runtime dependencies. The page lets students explore image manipulation techniques discussed in the lectures.

## Lecture 4 demos

The **Filter banks & texture** sidebar section contains three separate Lecture 4 demonstrations: the existing Gabor filter-bank demo with prepared parameter comparisons, automatic scale selection in sunflower and sky-lantern scenes, and a material classifier built from real grass, wood, and knitted-fabric photographs. The scale-selection page computes normalized Difference-of-Gaussians responses at eight scales, marks local maxima in space and scale, uses a shared colour range for the response-map comparison, and lets the user resize the source image to observe the corresponding shift in characteristic scale. The classifier pools the mean absolute response of each Gabor filter into a 16-dimensional texture vector, fits PCA on 24 independent training photographs (eight per class), and compares 1-nearest-neighbour with nearest-class-mean decisions for patches from separate test photographs. All filtering, feature extraction, PCA, and classification calculations run locally in the browser.

## Lecture 7 demos

The **Local features** sidebar section follows "07. Local Features I: Keypoints". The Harris corner detector runs on whichever image is loaded in the toolbar, or on one of the photographs in its image strip, and shows the four steps of the summary slide: Sobel gradients, the Gaussian-weighted second-moment matrix M with cornerness R = det(M) − κ·trace(M)², the threshold on R, and non-maximum suppression. Clicking the result or any step image shows M, its eigenvalues, and R for that window, and places the window in the eigenvalue plane. A second view rotates, rescales, or changes the contrast of the image, reruns the detector with the first view's absolute threshold, and reports repeatability, pairing corners one to one within 3 px; both views are drawn at the same pixel scale. The page's Python sample (SciPy Sobel and Gaussian filters with replicated borders, then scikit-image's `corner_peaks`) reproduces the page's corners exactly when given the same 480-px working copy.

The non-maximum suppression page (slide 25) runs greedy NMS on the real Harris response of a 5 × 5 checkerboard, whose 16 interior X-junctions are the true corners. The response is clipped at zero and rescaled to integers 0–99. Each iteration keeps the largest remaining value and zeroes its window, and the zoomed blob shows the values, kept maxima, suppression windows, and zeroed cells as Step or Play advances. Two fixed cases show the response before and after NMS: a 3 × 3 window leaves blob shoulders that become duplicates, and a 49 × 49 window erases genuine neighboring corners. With the default stop threshold, windows from 7 × 7 to 39 × 39 return exactly the 16 corners. The module lives in `js/nms.js`.

The adaptive non-maximum suppression page (slide 26) compares the strongest N Harris corners with the N corners that have the largest suppression radii, r_i = min ‖x_i − x_j‖ over candidates with R_j > c·R_i, for N and 2N side by side. Each ANMS panel reports the smallest kept radius r and the share of an 8 × 6 grid that the keypoints cover. Clicking a point draws its radius and the stronger keypoint that sets it. On the same 480-px working image, the page's Python sample reproduces both selections and r exactly.

## Lecture 8 demos

The **Descriptors & matching** section follows "08. Local Features II: Descriptors". Both pages use `js/sift.js`, a browser implementation of SIFT that follows OpenCV step by step but skips OpenCV's initial 2× upsampling for speed. `scripts/verify_sift_opencv.py` checks it against `cv2.SIFT`: with upsampling enabled it finds the same number of keypoints, 98.5–98.9% of them match one to one, and their descriptors agree within 1 of 255.

The SIFT descriptor page (slides 16 and 24–28) shows where a keypoint's orientation θ comes from, then follows the keypoint through the four descriptor steps: the scale- and orientation-normalized window, the Gaussian-weighted gradients on a 16 × 16 grid, the 4 × 4 × 8 orientation histograms, and normalization with clamping at 0.2 (adjustable, or off). A second section rotates and zooms the scene, re-detects SIFT keypoints, and checks whether the corresponding descriptor is still the nearest neighbour, for the selected keypoint and for the whole scene, with and without orientation normalization.

The ratio test page (slides 32–37) matches up to 500 of the strongest SIFT keypoints between a photograph and a second view made from it with a known homography and a lighting change, so every match is graded against its true location: it is correct when it lies within max(4 px, σ′/2) of that location and its scale is within 1.6× of the expected one. Moving the ratio threshold τ shows which matches are accepted or rejected, the TP/FP/FN/TN counts with precision, recall and specificity, the distribution of the ratio for correct and wrong nearest neighbours as on slide 33, and ROC curves for the ratio test and a plain distance threshold. An inspector shows the query f₁ beside its nearest (f₂) and second-nearest (f₂′) candidates. The UI is in `js/lecture8.js`.

## Lecture 10 demo

The **Stereo & depth** section follows "10. Depth Estimation & 3D Reconstruction", slides 27 and 28. It uses the lecture's own Middlebury 2014 Motorcycle pair at quarter size (741 × 500), as on slides 21, 25, 27 and 28, and the Middlebury 2005 Laundry scene (third size, seven equally spaced views) for five real baselines of 40–200 mm. For every pixel the page computes the SSD over a w × w window at every shift d (slide 25) and picks the lowest cost. Clicking a pixel draws its cost curve with the true disparity from Middlebury's ground truth and the chosen one, as on slide 27, and checks the four assumptions the slide lists. The case buttons jump to an example of each case: the slide's own pixels on the whiteboard, on the bench slats and behind the handlebar, and a pixel on the same headlight; at the slide's 9 × 9 window the whiteboard and handlebar pixels give the slide's chosen and true disparities, and the headlight pixel the slide's chosen 46 (true 58.4 against the slide's 58.5). The second section minimises slide 28's energy in the quadratic, confidence-weighted form described in the slide's notes, with uniform and edge-aware weights; with the slide's settings the page's re-implementation of the recipe in the slide's notes gives mean errors of 4.71, 3.77 and 3.41 px (the slide reports 4.7, 3.7 and 3.4; its exact image preprocessing is not documented). `js/stereo.js` holds the algorithms and `js/lecture10.js` the page. `scripts/verify_stereo.py` checks the JavaScript against an independent NumPy/SciPy implementation, and `scripts/build_stereo_assets.py` rebuilds `images/stereo/` from the Middlebury downloads.

## Lecture 12 · Tracking labs

- **Live MOSSE vs. NCC tracker** — draw a box on the webcam; a fixed NCC template and a MOSSE filter run side by side with live response maps and PSR. The camera needs HTTPS or `localhost` (GitHub Pages is fine); video never leaves the browser. A *Recorded clips* source replays three short real clips from the OTB-100 tracking benchmark (Wu, Lim and Yang, TPAMI 2015) with their ground-truth boxes — Dudek (head turn), Woman (occlusion), Walking2 (walk past) and Shaking (learning rate η) — in `images/tracking/`, for when no camera is available.
- **Data association exercise** — two real moments from the PETS 2009 sequence on the lecture slides (via MOTChallenge MOT15, CC BY-NC-SA 3.0; images in `images/association/`): frame t shows the tracked people, the next frame shows the public detections, and students drag track IDs onto detections before comparing with the minimum-cost (Hungarian) matching under last-position and constant-velocity costs.

Code lives in `js/tracking/`. Checks:

- `node --test tests/*.test.mjs` — FFT, trackers and association logic.
- Serve the repository root (`python3 -m http.server`) and open `/tests/clip-check.html` — replays the four recorded clips frame by frame and checks that the default settings still produce the outcomes the lecture describes. Re-run it after changing the tracker parameters.

## Bundled images

The thumbnail picker reads `images/manifest.json`. To add or remove an image, place it in `images/` and update the JSON array.

The stereo images in `images/stereo/` come from the [Middlebury stereo datasets](https://vision.middlebury.edu/stereo/data/): Motorcycle from D. Scharstein et al., "High-resolution stereo datasets with subpixel-accurate ground truth", GCPR 2014, and Laundry from D. Scharstein and C. Pal, "Learning conditional random fields for stereo", CVPR 2007, and H. Hirschmüller and D. Scharstein, "Evaluation of cost functions for stereo matching", CVPR 2007.

## Gabor filter-bank textures

The Lecture 4 Gabor demo uses five bundled texture images. Following the scikit-image reference method, each grayscale texture is standardized before filtering and the response is the phase-invariant power of the complex kernel at its original amplitude. Gamma = 1 reproduces the supplied circular-envelope reference; the gamma control extends it with a vertical envelope aspect ratio. Every power map uses one fixed display range, and the kernel preview uses a fixed ±40-pixel spatial frame so orientation changes cannot appear as zoom. `brick.png` and `grass.png` are CC0 samples distributed with [scikit-image](https://scikit-image.org/docs/stable/auto_examples/features_detection/plot_gabor.html); knit uses the supplied course attachment, while the zebra and corridor samples were already bundled with this course project.

The Lecture 4 demos additionally use `images/lecture4/sunflower.webp`, the supplied `images/lecture4/knit.png` teaching asset, two locally bundled Wikimedia Commons scale-selection scenes under `images/lecture4/scale/`, and 27 Wikimedia Commons photographs under `images/lecture4/materials/`. Source and licence links are shown directly on the relevant demo page.
